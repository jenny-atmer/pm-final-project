- **Strategic crisis, based on the brief, if the company does nothing for 12 months, what happens to its market position?:** Short answer: the brief points to slow erosion, not a sudden crisis. Riverty keeps its merchant business, but the problems it already has get harder to fix. The brief gives no market share figures, so this is reasoning from what it says, not a forecast.

Merchant-first growth does nothing for any of these. Meanwhile Klarna already runs a card on its own licence, and the brief says the gap is most visible in Germany. - **Moment of misery, what is the user likely doing outside the product (Google, spreadsheets, a competitor) because your tool isn’t serving them?:** The brief has no observed behaviour data, so these are hypotheses built from Lena's profile. The fact that she "prefers the money to leave her account only after she has decided to keep the item" is the best clue to where the misery sits.

1. Tracking what she owes across providers (strongest hypothesis). Lena uses BNPL on most non-trivial purchases and splits them across Klarna, PayPal and Riverty, depending on what appeared at checkout. With a middling income and no cushion, she probably keeps a note, a spreadsheet, a calendar reminder or an email search to see what falls due when relative to payday. No single provider can show her the whole picture, and that gap is a card opportunity only if Riverty can own more of her spend.

2. Juggling return windows against payment dates. Her whole motive is paying only after deciding to keep the item. She is probably checking return deadlines against due dates, waiting on refunds to show up, and chasing merchant or support channels when they don't. Any of that is a sign the product helps her defer payment but not follow through on the "keep or return" decision.

3. Falling back to Klarna or PayPal where Riverty isn't accepted. At a non-partner merchant or in a store, she opens whichever app works, or pays with a debit card and loses the deferral she wanted. The brief lists exactly this as Riverty's first gap, so the competitor is doing the job Riverty can't.

4. Googling terms when cash is short. If a due date arrives and money is tight, she is likely searching for how to split or extend a payment, and may land on PayPal Pay in 3, an instalment card like Santander Zero, or an overdraft. That is where Flex's interest-bearing role could matter, but only if she thinks of Riverty at that moment, and at 6% brand preference she probably doesn't.

5. Comparing options at checkout. When several BNPL options appear, she may open a tab to check fees, limits or terms. That is a small moment of friction, and it shows Riverty isn't her default.

How to test cheaply: interview five to eight heavy users and ask them to show their phone and walk through their last three BNPL purchases and the last time they were unsure what they owed. Alongside that, look at support contact reasons for returns and due-date questions, and at Flex drop-off points. If the workaround in #1 or #2 shows up repeatedly, that is the problem the first card should solve, rather than only matching Klarna's features.

Why the "new value" is credible, and what to validate:

New value: Riverty moves from being one option at checkout to being the place where Lena's deferred spending lives. The card extends "decide first, pay later" to stores and non-partner merchants (gap 1), gives heavy users a reason to consolidate (gap 2) and builds a direct relationship (gap 3).
Why now: Klarna's German lead is the clearest signal. The Mastercard membership and Paymentology platform already exist, funded by the Amazon Business pilot, so the marginal cost of a consumer slot is lowest now, before sequencing defaults to other workstreams.

Assumption to test first: that Lena actually wants consolidation and pay-after-keep on a card. The brief only tells us what she says about paying after deciding to keep an item, so the interviews suggested earlier should confirm this before it becomes the headline promise.

## Cold-read your own hook
- **Is the business risk high enough to justify a new initiative, a high-stakes threat, or a minor inconvenience?:** a real strategic threat, but not yet an emergency. It justifies a lean, tightly scoped initiative, not a large bet.
- **Is the moment of misery a systemic problem or just an edge case?:** likely systemic for heavy users, but not proven. Treat it as a strong hypothesis until discovery confirms it.
- **Does the value proposition actually remove the obstacle you identified, or is it a generic feature?:** Verdict: partly. The proposition removes one obstacle for real, but most of it reads as generic feature language, and one claim overreaches.

I've mapped each obstacle to what the proposition delivers:

Obstacle	Does the proposition remove it?
Riverty doesn't work in stores or at non-partner merchants	Yes, but it's table stakes. Klarna's card already does this, so it earns a place on Lena's phone but doesn't give her a reason to choose it.
Tracking what she owes across Klarna, PayPal and Riverty	Not as written. A card only sees what is paid with it, not her Klarna or PayPal balances.
Juggling return windows against due dates	Possibly, but the proposition doesn't say how. It lists "return windows and due dates in one place" without a mechanism.

1. The consolidation claim overreaches. "All deferred payments in one place" implies a view across providers, which a Riverty card can't give without account aggregation (open banking, for example). What it can do is let Lena move spend onto Riverty so there is less to track. That is displacement, and it only works if she chooses the card over Klarna or PayPal at checkout, which is exactly what she doesn't do today. So the card doesn't remove her workaround unless it wins the spend first.

2. "Pay only after deciding to keep" is the differentiated idea, but it's undefined. Whether a card purchase stays pending until the return window closes, and how that works with interchange, refunds, credit policy and the regulatory approval, is unspecified. The brief's CCD2 written-form blocker shows how much deferred-payment mechanics can constrain what is allowed. If the card simply charges at purchase like a normal credit card, the proposition collapses into "a Riverty-branded card".

3. The "because" is about Riverty's urgency, not Lena's. Klarna's lead, the 6% preference and regulatory lead time are business reasons. A strong proposition also says why Lena should act now, and I haven't given one.

A sharper version would drop the "everything in one place" promise and commit to one obstacle:

For heavy BNPL users like Lena who want money to leave their account only after they've decided to keep an item, we will offer a card that works anywhere and holds each purchase until her return window closes, with one clear date and amount for what's due. Because today she gets that only at the merchants that offer her BNPL, and has to track the rest herself.

Test before you commit: ask whether, after using the card for a month, Lena could drop her spreadsheet or reminder. If the answer is "only for purchases on the card," then the proposition is a better checkout option, not a fix for her cross-provider tracking. That is still worth building, but it should be sold as that.

Cheap way to check: a concierge test with a handful of heavy users, where they pay with a prototype card and you manually manage the hold-until-keep logic and the due-date view. If they keep using it over Klarna and PayPal, the proposition holds. If they use it only where nothing else works, it is a generic acceptance feature.
