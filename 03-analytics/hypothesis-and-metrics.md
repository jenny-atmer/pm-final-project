- **Problem Hook , your one-sentence framing of the business crisis (M1):** Riverty's users like Lena juggle several BNPL apps and pay at the till with someone else's card, while Klarna and PayPal grow into the stores and checkouts Riverty can't reach, and with a brand preference of only 6%, every month without a card moves Riverty closer to being a replaceable checkout button.
- **Value Proposition , the outcome your initiative promised to deliver (M1):** Value proposition: For busy BNPL users like Lena, who want money to leave their account only after they've decided to keep what they bought, the Riverty card lets them pay later anywhere, in stores and online, with one clear view of what's due. Unlike Klarna's debit-first card or PayPal's separate instalment products, it keeps the "decide first, pay after" habit in every setting and sends users back to Riverty's partner merchants instead of away from them. New value: Riverty moves from a checkout option Lena doesn't prefer to an everyday card she carries. That closes the in-store and heavy-user gaps from the brief and gives Riverty a direct consumer relationship for the first time. Why now: Klarna's card base is growing, and PayPal now offers instalments at Amazon.de and a virtual card for in-store payments, so Lena's habits are being set by competitors. From 20 November 2026, most BNPL falls under consumer credit rules, and Riverty carries that compliance cost with or without a card. Riverty's banking licence and Mastercard platform are already in place, and a consumer card is the next product that can earn that investment. The merchant benefit is a hypothesis, not something the brief establishes, so it should be one of the first things tested in discovery.

## Step 3 · Craft your hypothesis
- **Qualitative evidence (from M2) , quote the specific friction / moment of misery for your persona:** She knows Riverty, but doesn't prefer it and rarely goes looking for it.
- **Quantitative evidence (from M3) , name the metric or data point that confirms the pain; cite the number:** 6%. That is Riverty's brand preference in e-commerce checkouts.
- **Persona , role, goal, and the friction you confirmed in the reconciliation steps:** Persona: Lena, 32, Cologne

Role: A salaried, phone-first shopper on a middling income with no financial cushion, who uses BNPL on most non-trivial purchases and takes whichever option appears at checkout. [Brief]

Goal: To have money leave her account only after she has decided to keep the item. [Brief]

Friction: She knows Riverty but doesn't prefer it and rarely goes looking for it, so she can only pay later where a partner merchant happens to offer Riverty, and Klarna and PayPal fill the gaps.
- **Problem you are solving , one sentence describing the specific friction this initiative removes:** Lena can only pay later where a partner merchant offers Riverty, and because she doesn't prefer it, Riverty loses her to whichever BNPL option or card appears when it's absent.
- **Strategic outcome , what behaviour change do you expect, and how does it map to retention / revenue / churn?:** Expected behaviour change: Lena stops taking whichever BNPL option appears at checkout and starts reaching for the Riverty card first, online and in store, for the purchases where she wants to decide before she pays. On payday she checks one Riverty view for her card spend, instead of rebuilding it across apps.
- **Primary success metric (initiative signal) , the leading indicator that tells you the gap is closing:** Lena uses a Riverty card for purchases where she wants to decide before she pays, including at merchants and shops that don't offer Riverty today, so Riverty becomes something she carries, not a button that may appear.

Expected behaviour change: she picks Riverty first instead of "whichever option appears at checkout", online and in store. The files give no evidence of how she would behave with a card, so this is a hypothesis to test.
- **Guardrail metric (product signal) , the metric that must NOT drop; it protects your existing base:** Riverty checkout transactions at partner merchants. Track them among card holders against a comparable group of non-holders, and they must not fall once someone gets the card. This is the product signal that the card is adding to the merchant-first model, not replacing it.
- **Decision window , how much time or data before you scale, pivot, or kill? minimum threshold to proceed?:** Scale if activation reaches the brief's modeled 70% or higher, the repeat-use target above is met, and the guardrail holds.
Pivot if activation is fine but repeat use is weak. That would mean users like the card but it isn't winning the everyday purchase.
Kill if activation falls below about 50%, or if the volume cannibalized from integrated checkout costs more margin than the card earns.
- **Draft your full hypothesis sentence , one to three sentences; quote the metric, name the persona, name the outcome:** Based on Riverty's 6% brand preference at checkout, Lena's habit of taking whichever option appears, and 2023 interviews where flexible pay-later was among the most desired features and 9 of 10 rejected consumer credit, I believe that giving Lena a Riverty card she can use where Riverty isn't offered today will make Riverty a card she carries and uses first for purchases she wants to decide on, as measured by a 70% activation rate among approved users. I will protect Riverty checkout transactions at partner merchants among card holders, against a matched group of non-holders, and will make a go/no-go decision after [decision window, to be set before launch], scaling at 70% or higher with the repeat-use target met and the guardrail holding, and killing below about 50% or if cannibalised margin exceeds what the card earns.
